## A -- Success criteria and the fly-by pathology

**Bottom line:** The literature contains direct evidence that instantaneous threshold criteria can certify pass-through behavior, but only limited evidence that changing the training criterion alone causally changes a learned policy from passing-through to arriving. The strongest training-time evidence is Chen et al. Visual Dexterity: orientation-only success led to overshoot/oscillation, so they made the training success signal require orientation plus small finger and object motion. DeXtreme gives a very close held-goal evaluation, with consecutive successes dropping from 38.4 at N=0 to 27.3 at N=20, but it explicitly says the policy was not trained to hold, so it is evaluation-only evidence.

### Evidence on sweeping / passing-through policies

DeXtreme directly acknowledges the pass-through ambiguity in its held-goal evaluation: in the basic experiment, "the cube may shoot past the target"; the authors add an N-frame evaluation counter so the goal is not counted by chance. This is not a training intervention: they say the policy was "not trained explicitly to hold", and the footnote says fully stationary hold would require "zero velocities at the target" and "changing the reward function." Quantitatively, the held-goal simulation table confirms the prompt numbers: `N=0: 38.4`, `N=5: 35.3`, `N=10: 33.3`, `N=20: 27.3`; their explanation for the `N=20` drop is that the LSTM "was not trained for such scenarios."

Chen et al. Visual Dexterity is the closest training-time analogue to our fly-by finding. They state that an orientation-only criterion led to overshooting and oscillation in the real world, and they changed the success definition used during training to include small finger and object motion. They also explicitly characterize OpenAI-style reorientation as counting "passes through a target pose" and say prior controllers were never trained to stop at the target pose, which they "experimentally found harder to learn." This is evidence that the success definition can shape arrival behavior, but the paper does not give a clean A/B table with identical policies trained only under instantaneous vs settled rewards.

Chen et al. General In-Hand Reorientation reports a different dynamic strategy: the agent "throws the object in the air with a spin" and catches it when orientation matches. That is not the same pathology as uncontrolled fly-by, because the paper frames it as a successful emergent strategy under a single-goal termination task. It still matters because the reward is instantaneous threshold-based (`theta_bar = 0.1 rad`) and permits dynamic arrival rather than requiring a settled stop.

AnyRotate and Text2Touch are continuous-rotation papers, so passing through subgoals is structurally intended: the environment updates the target after a goal tolerance is met and continues rotating. Their relevant evidence is not fly-by pathology but the absence of stillness gates in a task where continuous motion is desired.

OpenAI Dactyl and OpenAI Rubik's Cube ADR use instantaneous goal achievement and immediate goal sequencing. I found no passage in either paper saying the hand pauses at a goal before sampling the next one, and no passage diagnosing overshoot/fly-by as a failure mode. Rubik's ADR discusses getting stuck, wrong-face rotations, and slipping; those are recovery/failure modes, not pass-through-arrival evidence.

### What this predicts for us

Our measured result matches the literature pattern: an instantaneous one-step criterion can make "being within tolerance once" sufficient, so a policy that sweeps through SO(3) can score without arriving. DeXtreme's own held evaluation says an untrained policy can shoot past the target; our stricter local measurement quantifies the same kind of event at `0.1 rad`, with closest-approach cube speed `0.85 rad/s` and only `3.1%` stopped at closest approach.

Visual Dexterity predicts that adding a settled success criterion into the reward, not merely into evaluation, is the most principled change if the desired behavior is arrival. Their settled reward requires orientation plus low motion; the values we should treat as a literature anchor are `qdot_bar = 0.25`, `v_bar = 0.04`, and `omega_bar = 0.5`, with the important caveat that their real-world deployment relaxed the object-motion part because object velocity was hard to measure from point clouds.

DeXtreme predicts that post hoc held evaluation alone will reduce reported successes but will not necessarily create a holding policy. Their N-frame table drops only moderately from `38.4` to `33.3` at `N=10`, but to `27.3` at `N=20`; they attribute the large-N drop to the LSTM not being trained for such scenarios. For our environment, a held metric is diagnostic, while a held/still reward or delayed-goal-refresh during training is the likely behavior-shaping intervention.

### Confidence and gaps

High confidence: the seven source files show that OpenAI Dactyl, OpenAI ADR block reorientation, DeXtreme training, Chen General, AnyRotate, and Text2Touch all use instantaneous threshold/subgoal crossing for the reward-side success event, while Visual Dexterity uses a settled compound training success criterion. High confidence: DeXtreme's `N=0/5/10/20` values are exactly `38.4/35.3/33.3/27.3`, and the paper explicitly says the policy was not trained to hold.

Medium confidence on causal strength: Visual Dexterity provides direct qualitative evidence that orientation-only success caused overshoot/oscillation and that adding motion criteria addressed it, but it is not a controlled ablation table isolating only the success predicate. DeXtreme provides quantitative held-goal evaluation and a "shoot past" passage, but because the held condition is evaluation-only, it does not prove that training on a held criterion would change learned behavior.

No such passage found: I found no Dactyl, OpenAI ADR, AnyRotate, or Text2Touch passage reporting a policy that unexpectedly sweeps/passes through a target and then fixes it via a reward criterion change. The absence matters because continuous-rotation work intentionally rewards repeated subgoal passage, whereas our task asks for a final orientation and currently rewards a single instantaneous threshold entry.
