## B -- What happens to the goal after a success

**Bottom line:** I found no working system in this set that uses a rule like our active MJX path: immediately injecting a large angular-velocity kick into the goal and then integrating that moving goal away from the object. The closest OpenAI-family systems do advance immediately after success, but by sampling a new target, not by continuously drifting the old target; DeXtreme explicitly adds an `N=10` frame hold test before refresh; DexNDM and AnyRotate use waypoint/auxiliary-goal rules for continuous rotation rather than a stop-and-hold reorientation benchmark. The measured result in `research/FINDING.md` is therefore highly consistent with the mechanism: "Under drift it does not, because the instant it arrives the goal kicks ~160° away -- so stopping is worthless and sweeping is optimal."

### The playground paper-versus-code discrepancy

The MuJoCo Playground paper text and the dumped code disagree on the central mechanism. The paper says a fixed target is resampled only after reaching it, with a minimum angular separation: "Upon reaching a target orientation within a 0.4 rad tolerance, a new orientation is sampled and the success counter is incremented" and "new orientations are sampled at least 90° away from the previous goal." The code, however, increments success and then moves the existing mocap goal by a decaying integrated angular velocity: `state.info["goal_quat_dquat"] = jp.where(success, 3 + jax.random.uniform(goal_rng, (3,), minval=-2, maxval=2), state.info["goal_quat_dquat"] * 0.8)` followed by `goal_quat = math.quat_integrate(state.data.mocap_quat[0], state.info["goal_quat_dquat"], 2 * jp.array(self.dt))`.

Our port reproduces the code, not the paper statement. The corresponding local implementation is `kick = sample_uniform(1.0, 5.0, (self.num_envs, 3), device=self.device)`, `self.goal_dquat * 0.8`, and `_quat_integrate(self.quat_command_w, self.goal_dquat, 2.0 * float(self._env.step_dt))`. Because `orientation_success_threshold: float = 0.1` and `use_mjx_goal_drift: bool = True`, the active reference config uses the drift rule.

### What this predicts for us

No quoted working system here uses exactly our rule: an immediate large goal-motion kick on success followed by continuous decay/integration of the goal. OpenAI and DeXtreme give the next goal immediately, but as a new target orientation; DeXtreme's hold variant deliberately attacks fly-bys by requiring `N=10` consecutive in-threshold frames before refresh. AnyRotate and DexNDM are continuous-rotation systems, and their goal updates are waypoint/auxiliary-goal mechanisms aligned with the desired rotation axis; they should not be treated as evidence that an arbitrary far-away post-success drift is benign for a reorientation benchmark.

The measured behavior in this repo is exactly the pathology DeXtreme's hold benchmark warns about: `research/FINDING.md` reports that "episodes actually stopped there (|ω| < 0.2)" are `3.1%` for XELA and `3.4%` for BARE, while "SAME checkpoint, goal pinned instead" changes `HELD @ 0.1 rad for 10 consecutive steps` from `~0% to 78%`. It also reports `|ω| over the last 50% of the episode` at `1.14 rad/s` for XELA, `1.18 rad/s` for BARE, and says `96.9%` of arrivals are fly-bys in the task prompt. That is direct within-policy evidence that this goal-advancement rule changes whether the policy arrives or sweeps.

If we changed the kick to the Playground paper's stated rule, the literature would predict a different benchmark: a sequential reorientation task with immediate fixed-goal resampling and a minimum separation, closer to OpenAI/DeXtreme basic goal reaching. It would probably still reward fast transitions between goals, and with no dwell it could still count fly-throughs, but it would remove the special incentive to keep chasing a continuously moving target the instant it is touched. If we added a dwell requirement before advancing, DeXtreme predicts fewer peak consecutive successes but a more meaningful success signal: "the maximum consecutive successes are only 45 as opposed to 112" under hold, yet "the average consecutive successes have not dropped as dramatically"; their stated reason is that "holding the cube for N frames in a row ensures that the goal was not achieved by chance."

It would not be the same benchmark in either case. Uniform resample with a `90°` minimum separation would match the Playground paper text better than the Playground code and our port; adding dwell would create a stricter hold-capable reorientation benchmark closer to DeXtreme's robustness test. The most directly relevant quantitative prediction from our own measurement is that pinning/holding reveals latent capability: `|ω| at best falls 0.85 -> 0.35 rad/s`, `|ω| late falls 1.14 -> 0.57`, and hold success rises to `78%`.

### Confidence and gaps

High confidence on the code-level finding: the Playground dump and our implementation contain the same `3 + U(-2,2)` / `* 0.8` / `quat_integrate(..., 2 * dt)` pattern. High confidence that OpenAI Dactyl, OpenAI ADR, DeXtreme, AnyRotate, DexNDM, and From Simple to Complex do not quote a mechanism identical to our integrated post-success drift in the requested source text.

The main gap is that `repo_mujoco_playground_leap_hand.txt` itself contains only the Playground code dump, not the paper sentence about `90°` separation; that exact paper quote comes from the separate local source `research/sources/mujoco_playground_paper__arxiv_2502.08844.txt`. I did not find a post-success dwell or pause in OpenAI Dactyl/ADR, AnyRotate, DexNDM, or From Simple to Complex; where the source did not specify one, the table says "not specified" rather than inferring absence from architecture alone.
